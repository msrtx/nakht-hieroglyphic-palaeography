Visual and Written Culture

Scribes and Artists

The creation of funerary manuscripts required specialised training. Nakht himself held elite status as royal scribe, suggesting access to skilled workshop personnel familiar with canonical forms. The quality of execution suggests training under master scribes familiar with canonical forms.

The roles of scribe (sš) and artist were sometimes combined in a single artisan, particularly for high-status commissions. The careful integration of text and image in this manuscript suggests a coordinated workshop effort.

Cursive vs. Monumental Context

Egyptian writing systems employed different script styles depending on context:

ContextCharacteristics
Cursive (Papyrus)Faster execution, simplified forms, adapted to brush/reed pen on flexible surface
Monumental (Stone)Carefully carved, formalised proportions, often painted, intended for permanence

This papyrus represents the cursive tradition while maintaining fidelity to monumental standards — a hallmark of high-quality New Kingdom funerary manuscripts.

The Book of the Dead of Nakht occupies a unique position between the rapid cursive of daily administrative writing and the careful monumental carving of temple inscriptions.
