Dating and provenance

This funerary manuscript was created for Nakht, royal scribe and military commander during the Late Eighteenth Dynasty (~1350 BCE), Thebes.

Dating Evidence

Palaeographic analysis places this manuscript in the Late Eighteenth Dynasty (c. 1390–1352 BCE), based on handwriting style consistent with Tell el-Amarna period manuscripts.

Graphic registers

The manuscript is organized into vertical registers (columns) with clear spacing between sections. Each register contains hieroglyphic signs arranged right-to-left, following standard Egyptian convention.

The layout demonstrates careful planning with larger hieroglyphs for divine names and standardized spacing for regular passages.

Stroke order

Egyptian hieroglyphic writing follows specific conventions for stroke execution and sign construction. Scribes were trained to produce consistent forms using particular brush or reed pen techniques.

Observations from this manuscript indicate careful execution with attention to proportional balance within each sign.

Scribes and artists

The creation of funerary manuscripts involved specialised artisans. Nakht himself held the title of royal scribe and military commander, indicating elite status and likely access to skilled workshop personnel.

The quality of execution suggests training under master scribes familiar with canonical forms.

Cursive vs. Monumental context

Egyptian writing systems employed different script styles depending on context:

Context Characteristics
Cursive (Papyrus) Faster execution, simplified forms, adapted to brush/reed pen on flexible surface
Monumental (Stone) Carefully carved, formalised proportions, often painted, intended for permanence

This papyrus represents the cursive tradition while maintaining fidelity to monumental standards.
